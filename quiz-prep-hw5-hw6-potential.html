2 · The two ideas, from zero

2.1 Work, and why a "potential energy" exists at all

Work is force times the distance moved along the force: \(W = Fd\cos\theta\), in joules. When a force helps the motion (\(\theta \lt 90^\circ\)) its work is positive; when it fights the motion its work is negative. The work-energy theorem from Physics 2111 says the total work by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy, \(W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K\).

Some forces, gravity and the Coulomb force among them, have a special property: the work they do between two points does not depend on the path. For such a force you can define a potential energy \(U\) that only depends on where things are, with

\[ \Delta U = U_{\text{final}} - U_{\text{initial}} = -W_{\text{field}} \]
What each letter means
\(W\)
work done by a force, in joules (J)
\(F,\ d,\ \theta\)
force magnitude (N), distance moved (m), angle between the force and the motion
\(\Delta K\)
change in kinetic energy, \(\tfrac12mv_f^2 - \tfrac12mv_i^2\), in J
\(U,\ \Delta U\)
electric potential energy and its change (final minus initial), in J
\(W_{\text{field}}\)
work done by the electric force (not by your hand), in J
Why the minus sign

Lift a ball. Gravity pulls down while the ball moves up, so gravity's work is negative, and the ball's potential energy goes up. Same rule for charges: when the electric force does negative work (the charge is pushed against the field's wishes), \(U\) increases; when the field does positive work (the charge goes where the field pushes it), \(U\) decreases. Lecture 5's slogan: "Nature wants things to move in such a way that PE decreases." A charge released from rest always starts moving in the direction that lowers \(U\).

Your hand is different. It is not a conservative force, so it has no potential energy of its own. When you carry a charge at constant speed, \(\Delta K = 0\), so your work exactly cancels the field's: \(W_{\text{hand}} = -W_{\text{field}} = +\Delta U\). Whatever energy you put in is stored as \(U\).

2.2 Potential energy of two point charges

\[ U = \frac{k\,q_1 q_2}{r} \qquad (U = 0 \text{ when the charges are infinitely far apart}) \]
What each letter means
\(U\)
the potential energy stored in the pair, in J; it belongs to the system, not to one charge
\(k\)
Coulomb's constant, \(8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\)
\(q_1,\ q_2\)
the two charges with their signs, in coulombs (C); \(1\ \mu\mathrm{C} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(1\ \mathrm{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}\)
\(r\)
the distance between them, in meters; it is not squared (the force has \(r^2\), the energy has \(r\))
Why 1/r, and why the sign matters

The Coulomb force falls as \(1/r^2\). Potential energy is the work needed to bring the charges in from infinity, and adding up (integrating) a \(1/r^2\) force over distance gives \(1/r\). Keep the signs of the charges in the formula: a positive product means like charges (a hill, \(U \gt 0\)), a negative product means opposite charges (a well, \(U \lt 0\)). Lecture 5's clickers: two opposite charges at \(d\) have lower energy than at \(2d\) (deeper in the well), while two negative charges at \(d\) have higher energy than at \(2d\) (higher on the hill). "Highest potential energy" is the answer to both questions but for opposite reasons.

Change in \(U\) when one charge moves. If \(+q\) goes from distance \(r_1\) to distance \(r_2\) from a fixed \(+Q\):

\[ \Delta U = kQq\left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1}\right) \]

This is the Lecture 5 checkpoint (answer D). Only the starting and ending distances matter; the straight-line distance actually traveled, \(R\), never appears, because the force is conservative. Moving away (\(r_2 \gt r_1\)) makes \(\Delta U\) negative for like charges, as it must.

2.3 Many charges: one term per pair

\[ U_{\text{total}} = \sum_{\text{every pair}}\frac{k\,q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} \]
What each letter means
\(\sum_{\text{every pair}}\)
add one term for each pair of charges, counting each pair once: 3 charges make 3 pairs, 4 charges make 6 pairs
\(q_i,\ q_j\)
the two charges in that pair, with signs, in C
\(r_{ij}\)
the distance between those two charges, in m

\(U_{\text{total}}\) is the work an outside agent must do to assemble the whole arrangement from charges that start infinitely far apart. Lecture 5 builds it one charge at a time: the first charge costs nothing (nothing is there yet), the second costs its pair with the first, the third costs its pairs with both of the others, and so on. Two consequences you will use:

  • Bringing in one more charge \(q\) (the others already in place) changes \(U\) by the sum of the new pairs only, which is \(\Delta U = qV\) where \(V\) is the potential the existing charges make at the new spot (HW5 P1 part b, HW5 P5, Fall 2018 Q4).
  • Pairs between fixed charges never change while a third charge moves, so leave them out of any energy-conservation calculation (HW5 P6).

2.4 Energy conservation: the speed of a released charge

The Coulomb force is conservative, so when nothing else does work (no hand, no friction), mechanical energy is conserved:

\[ K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tfrac12 m v_f^2 = U_i - U_f \ \text{ (released from rest)} \]
What each letter means
\(K_i,\ K_f\)
initial and final kinetic energy, \(\tfrac12mv^2\), in J; \(K_i = 0\) when released from rest
\(U_i,\ U_f\)
potential energy at the starting and ending positions, from \(kQq/r\) (summed over the moving charge's pairs), in J
\(m\)
mass of the moving charge, in kg: \(1\ \mu\mathrm{g} = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg}\), \(1\ \mathrm{mg} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{kg}\)
\(v_f\)
its speed at the end, \(v_f = \sqrt{2(U_i - U_f)/m}\), in m/s

Steps that never change: (1) write \(U\) at the start, (2) write \(U\) at the end, (3) the difference is the kinetic energy gained, (4) \(v = \sqrt{2K/m}\). If the charge is released from rest and \(U_i - U_f\) comes out negative, you have the direction wrong: a charge released from rest can only move toward lower \(U\). Only when both charges are free (Lecture 5, Example 5.1) do you also need momentum conservation to split the energy between them.

2.5 Electric potential: energy per coulomb

The potential energy of a charge \(q\) in someone else's field is always proportional to \(q\). Divide it out and what remains is a property of the location alone, the electric potential:

\[ V = \frac{U}{q}, \qquad V_{\text{point charge}} = \frac{kq}{r}, \qquad V_{\text{several}} = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]
What each letter means
\(V\)
electric potential at a point, in volts (1 V = 1 J/C); a plain number, no direction
\(U\)
the potential energy a charge \(q\) has at that point, in J; so \(U = qV\)
\(q\) (in \(kq/r\))
the source charge making the potential, with its sign: a negative charge makes negative \(V\)
\(r,\ r_i\)
distance from the source charge to the point, in m
\(\sum_i\)
add the numbers \(kq_i/r_i\) for each source charge; no components, no angles ("no sines or cosines," Lecture 6)

The two relationships you will use constantly:

\[ \Delta U = q\,\Delta V, \qquad W_{\text{field}} = -q\,\Delta V, \qquad W_{\text{you}} = +q\,\Delta V \]
Why potential is useful, and how to read its sign

Field is force per coulomb; potential is energy per coulomb. Both describe the space, not the test charge. Around a positive charge \(V\) is a hill (positive, higher near the charge); around a negative charge it is a well. A positive charge rolls downhill, toward lower \(V\). A negative charge does the opposite: its energy \(qV\) is lowest where \(V\) is highest, so it moves toward higher \(V\). Lecture 6's Example 6.1 makes the point: flipping the sign of \(q\) flips the force, the works, and \(\Delta U\), but \(\Delta V\) stays \(+2.4\ \mathrm{V}\); the potential difference belongs to the field, not to the charge.

2.6 Equipotentials: same distance, same potential, zero work

All points at the same distance from a point charge have the same \(V\). Moving a charge between two such points changes nothing: \(\Delta V = 0\), so \(\Delta U = 0\) and the field does no work, no matter how long or curved the path. Surfaces of constant \(V\) are called equipotentials; for a point charge they are spheres, for a long line they are cylinders, for a large flat sheet they are planes. The field is always perpendicular to them and points from high \(V\) to low \(V\). A conductor in equilibrium has \(E = 0\) inside, so its entire volume is one equipotential (Lecture 6 checkpoint: zero field means constant \(V\), not zero \(V\)). Equipotential lines drawn at equal voltage steps are closest together where the field is strongest.

2.7 Potential from the field, and field from the potential

\[ V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \vec E\cdot d\vec l, \qquad \text{uniform field along the motion: } \Delta V = -Ed, \qquad E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx} \]
What each letter means
\(V_a,\ V_b\)
potential at the start and end points, in V
\(\vec E\)
the electric field along the path, in N/C = V/m
\(d\vec l\)
a tiny step along the path, in m; the dot product keeps the part of the step along \(\vec E\)
\(d\)
distance moved along the field direction, in m
\(E_x = -dV/dx\)
the field is minus the slope of \(V\); it points "downhill," from high \(V\) to low \(V\)

Walking with the field lowers \(V\); walking against it raises \(V\); walking perpendicular to it changes nothing. On a graph of \(V\) versus \(x\), the field is strongest where the curve is steepest, and it points in \(-x\) wherever the slope is positive (Lecture 6 checkpoints).

Why a conductor is flat in V, and why lines cannot use infinity

Inside a conductor in equilibrium the field is zero, and \(\Delta V = -\int E\,dr\) adds up nothing across it, so \(V\) holds the value it had at the outer surface all the way through (HW6 P1's copper shell, P2's aluminum interior). For an infinite line the field falls only as \(1/r\), and \(\int dr/r = \ln r\) grows without limit, so "zero at infinity" is impossible; the problem hands you a reference radius instead, and the potential at any \(r\) is minus the log of the ratio \(r/r_0\).

  1. Read the figure: signs and sides

    WhyThe problem prints only the sizes of the charges. The ⊕ and ⊖ drawn in the figure carry the signs, and the labels on the sides tell you which distance belongs to which pair.

    \(q_1 = +9.5\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (left corner), \(q_2 = -58\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (the right-angle corner, drawn ⊖), \(q_3 = +25\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (top corner).

    MeansTwo pairs have opposite signs (1–2 and 2–3), so they will give negative terms. One pair has like signs (1–3), so it will give a positive term. Knowing this in advance lets you catch a sign slip later.

  2. Convert everything to SI units

    Why\(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\) is built from coulombs and meters. If the charges are not in coulombs and the distances not in meters, the answer is off by powers of ten.

    "μ" means micro, \(\times10^{-6}\): \(q_1 = 9.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(q_2 = -58\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(q_3 = 25\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\).
    "c" means centi, \(\times10^{-2}\): \(a = 26\ \mathrm{cm}\) \(= 0.26\ \mathrm{m}\), \(b = 65\ \mathrm{cm}\) \(= 0.65\ \mathrm{m}\).

  3. Count the pairs

    WhyPotential energy belongs to pairs of charges, not to single charges. Each pair is counted once: "1 with 2" and "2 with 1" are the same pair.

    Three charges make \(\dfrac{3\times2}{2} = 3\) pairs: \((1,2)\), \((2,3)\), \((1,3)\).

    MeansPart (a) will be a sum of exactly three terms.

  4. Find the distance that is not printed

    Why\(q_1\) and \(q_3\) sit at the two ends of the hypotenuse. The right angle is at \(q_2\), so the Pythagorean theorem gives the hypotenuse from the two legs.

    \(r_{13} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\)
    \(= \sqrt{(0.26)^2 + (0.65)^2}\)
    \(= \sqrt{0.0676 + 0.423}\) \(= \sqrt{0.4901}\)

    Multiply the charges, plain numbers first, then powers of ten: \((9.5)(-58)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-6}\) \(= -551\times10^{-12}\) \(= -5.510\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)

    MeansNegative, because a positive and a negative charge attract.

  6. Pair 2–3: \(U_{23} = \dfrac{kq_2q_3}{b}\)

    WhySame equation, now with \(q_2\) and \(q_3\). The distance between them is side \(b\).

    Multiply by \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(-1.450\times10^{-9})\) \(= -13.04\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)
    Divide by the distance \(b = 0.650\ \mathrm{m}\): \(-13.04 \div 0.650\) \(= -20.05\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansNegative again: another attracting pair.

  7. Pair 1–3: \(U_{13} = \dfrac{kq_1q_3}{r_{13}}\)

    Why\(q_1\) and \(q_3\) are joined by the hypotenuse, so the distance is \(r_{13}\) from the Pythagoras step.

    Multiply the charges, plain numbers first, then powers of ten: \((9.5)(25)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-6}\) \(= 237.5\times10^{-12}\) \(= 2.375\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)
    Multiply by \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(2.375\times10^{-10})\) \(= 2.135\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)
    Divide by the distance \(r_{13} = 0.700\ \mathrm{m}\): \(2.135 \div 0.700\) \(= 3.050\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansPositive: two like charges repel, and energy is stored in pushing them together.

    WhyEnergy is a plain number with no direction. The total is just the sum of the pair terms, with their signs.

    \(U = U_{12} + U_{23} + U_{13}\)
    \(= (-19.05) + (-20.05) + (3.050)\)
    \(= -36.1\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansNegative: the attractions outweigh the repulsion. It would take \(36.1\ \mathrm{J}\) of outside work to pull all three charges apart to infinity.

    WhyBefore \(q_3\) arrives, \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are already sitting there, so their pair energy \(U_{12}\) already exists. \(q_3\) starts at infinity, where every pair it belongs to has zero energy. Bringing it in creates exactly the pairs that contain \(q_3\).

    WhyEvery answer depends on which region you are in, so first fix where the plates are and which one is the zero.

  2. The field of one plate by itself

    WhyThis is the given \(E_{\text{sheet}} = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0\). It does not weaken with distance, it is the same on both sides, and it points away from a positive sheet and toward a negative one.

(i) Equipotentials, true: the whole copper volume \(b \lt r \lt c\); spherical surfaces in the gap \(a \lt r \lt b\); the whole aluminum volume \(r \lt a\); spherical surfaces outside, \(r \gt c\). 1 · HW5 P4Quiz-style, new numbers

A point charge \(Q_1 = +6.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) is fixed in space, while a point charge \(Q_2 = +4.0\ \mathrm{nC}\) with mass \(8.0\ \mu\mathrm{g}\) is free to move. (a) Calculate the electric potential energy of the system when \(Q_2\) is \(0.30\ \mathrm{m}\) from \(Q_1\). (b) If \(Q_2\) is released from rest at \(0.30\ \mathrm{m}\), what is its speed when it is \(0.90\ \mathrm{m}\) from \(Q_1\)?

Equations provided \(U = \dfrac{kQ_1Q_2}{r}\) · \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\) · \(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\) · \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\)
SolutionBit by bit. Work it first, then open

  1. Read the setup

    WhyOnly the free charge can move, so only it can have kinetic energy. The fixed one never moves and never gets any.

    \(Q_1 = +6.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\): fixed in place.
    \(Q_2 = +4.0\ \mathrm{nC}\), mass \(m = 8.0\ \mu\mathrm{g}\): free to move.
    It starts at \(r_i = 0.30\ \mathrm{m}\) from \(Q_1\) and we want its speed at \(r_f = 0.90\ \mathrm{m}\).

    MeansBoth charges are positive, so they repel. \(Q_2\) is pushed away from \(Q_1\) and speeds up as it goes outward.

  2. Convert to SI units

    Why\(k\) works in coulombs and meters, and \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\) needs kilograms to give joules.

    "μ" is \(10^{-6}\): \(Q_1 = 6.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\).
    "n" (nano) is \(10^{-9}\): \(Q_2 = 4.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}\).
    A microgram is \(10^{-6}\ \mathrm{g}\), and a gram is \(10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}\), so \(1\ \mu\mathrm{g}\) \(= 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg}\): \(m = 8.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg}\).

    MeansGetting the mass wrong by a factor of 1000 is the most common way to lose this problem.

  3. Compute \(kQ_1Q_2\) once

    WhyThe top of \(U = kQ_1Q_2/r\) is the same at both distances. Work it out once and reuse it.

    Charges: \((6.0)(4.0)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-9}\) \(= 24\times10^{-15}\) \(= 2.400\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)
    Times \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(2.400\times10^{-14})\) \(= 2.158\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)

  4. Part (a): \(U\) at the starting distance

    WhyUse the given \(U = kQ_1Q_2/r\) with \(r = r_i\). Divide by \(r\); do not square it.

    \(U_i\) \(= \dfrac{2.158\times10^{-4}}{0.30}\) \(= 7.19\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansPositive, as it must be for two like charges. This stored energy is what will turn into motion.

  5. Part (b): write down energy conservation

    WhyNo outside force acts, so the total energy \(K + U\) stays the same. That is the given \(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\). \(K\) is the moving charge's kinetic energy; \(U\) is the pair's potential energy.

    \(K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f\)

  6. "Released from rest" means \(K_i = 0\)

    WhyAt rest, \(v = 0\), so \(K = \tfrac12m(0)^2 = 0\). The problem gives you this number in words, not symbols.

    \(0 + U_i = K_f + U_f\) \(\Rightarrow K_f\) \(= U_i - U_f\)

    MeansThe kinetic energy gained equals the potential energy lost.

  7. \(U\) at the final distance

    WhySame top, new distance \(r_f\).

    \(U_f\) \(= \dfrac{2.158\times10^{-4}}{0.90}\) \(= 2.40\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansSmaller than \(U_i\), because the charges are farther apart.

  8. Subtract to get the kinetic energy

    \(K_f\) \(= 7.19\times10^{-4} - 2.40\times10^{-4}\) \(= 4.79\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansPositive, so the outward motion really happens. A negative result would mean a sign slip.

  9. Solve \(K = \tfrac12mv^2\) for \(v\)

    WhyUndo each operation around \(v\) in reverse order: the ½, then the \(m\), then the square.

    \(K_f = \tfrac12mv^2\)
    Multiply both sides by 2: \(2K_f = mv^2\)
    Divide by \(m\): \(v^2 = \dfrac{2K_f}{m}\)
    Square root: \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2K_f}{m}}\)

  10. Plug in

    Top: \(2K_f = 2(4.79\times10^{-4})\) \(= 9.59\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J}\)
    Divide by the mass: \(\dfrac{9.59\times10^{-4}}{8.0\times10^{-9}}\) \(= 1.20\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\)
    Square root: \(v = \sqrt{1.20\times10^{5}}\) \(= 346\ \mathrm{m/s}\)

    MeansUnits check: J/kg is \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), and its square root is m/s.

(a) U = 7.19×10⁻⁴ J (b) v = 346 m/s
2 · HW5 P1Quiz-style, new numbers

Three point charges sit at the corners of a right triangle as shown: \(q_1 = 3.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\), \(q_2 = 9.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (negative, as drawn), \(q_3 = 5.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\). Equations provided \(U = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{r}\) · \(U_{\text{total}} = \sum_{\text{pairs}}\dfrac{kq_iq_j}{r_{ij}}\) · \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\)
SolutionBit by bit. Work it first, then open

  1. Read the figure: signs and sides

    WhyThe problem prints only the sizes of the charges. The ⊕ and ⊖ drawn in the figure carry the signs, and the labels on the sides tell you which distance belongs to which pair.

    \(q_1 = +3.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (left corner), \(q_2 = -9.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (the right-angle corner, drawn ⊖), \(q_3 = +5.0\ \mu\mathrm{C}\) (top corner).

    MeansTwo pairs have opposite signs (1–2 and 2–3), so they will give negative terms. One pair has like signs (1–3), so it will give a positive term. Knowing this in advance lets you catch a sign slip later.

  2. Convert everything to SI units

    Why\(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\) is built from coulombs and meters. If the charges are not in coulombs and the distances not in meters, the answer is off by powers of ten.

    "μ" means micro, \(\times10^{-6}\): \(q_1 = 3.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(q_2 = -9.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(q_3 = 5.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\).
    The sides are already in meters: \(a = 0.60\ \mathrm{m}\), \(b = 0.80\ \mathrm{m}\).

  3. Count the pairs

    WhyPotential energy belongs to pairs of charges, not to single charges. Each pair is counted once: "1 with 2" and "2 with 1" are the same pair.

    Three charges make \(\dfrac{3\times2}{2} = 3\) pairs: \((1,2)\), \((2,3)\), \((1,3)\).

    MeansPart (a) will be a sum of exactly three terms.

  4. Find the distance that is not printed

    Why\(q_1\) and \(q_3\) sit at the two ends of the hypotenuse. The right angle is at \(q_2\), so the Pythagorean theorem gives the hypotenuse from the two legs.

    \(r_{13} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\)
    \(= \sqrt{(0.60)^2 + (0.80)^2}\)
    \(= \sqrt{0.360 + 0.640}\) \(= \sqrt{1.000}\)
    \(= 1.00\ \mathrm{m}\)

    MeansIt is longer than either leg but shorter than \(a + b = 1.40\ \mathrm{m}\). Adding the legs is a common mistake.

  5. Pair 1–2: \(U_{12} = \dfrac{kq_1q_2}{a}\)

    WhyThis is the given \(U = kq_1q_2/r\) with the signs kept inside the charges. The distance between \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) is side \(a\). The distance is not squared: \(r^2\) belongs to the force law, not to the energy.

    Multiply the charges, plain numbers first, then powers of ten: \((3.0)(-9.0)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-6}\) \(= -27\times10^{-12}\) \(= -2.700\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)
    Multiply by \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(-2.700\times10^{-11})\) \(= -0.2427\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)

    MeansNegative, because a positive and a negative charge attract.

  6. Pair 2–3: \(U_{23} = \dfrac{kq_2q_3}{b}\)

    WhySame equation, now with \(q_2\) and \(q_3\). The distance between them is side \(b\).

    Multiply the charges, plain numbers first, then powers of ten: \((-9.0)(5.0)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-6}\) \(= -45\times10^{-12}\) \(= -4.500\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)
    Multiply by \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(-4.500\times10^{-11})\) \(= -0.4046\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)
    Divide by the distance \(b = 0.800\ \mathrm{m}\): \(-0.4046 \div 0.800\) \(= -0.5057\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansNegative again: another attracting pair.

  7. Pair 1–3: \(U_{13} = \dfrac{kq_1q_3}{r_{13}}\)

    Why\(q_1\) and \(q_3\) are joined by the hypotenuse, so the distance is \(r_{13}\) from the Pythagoras step.

    Multiply the charges, plain numbers first, then powers of ten: \((3.0)(5.0)\times10^{-6}\times10^{-6}\) \(= 15\times10^{-12}\) \(= 1.500\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{C^2}\)
    Multiply by \(k\): \((8.99\times10^{9})(1.500\times10^{-11})\) \(= 0.1348\ \mathrm{J\,m}\)
    Divide by the distance \(r_{13} = 1.00\ \mathrm{m}\): \(0.1348 \div 1.00\) \(= 0.1348\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansPositive: two like charges repel, and energy is stored in pushing them together.

  8. Add the three terms: this is part (a)

    WhyEnergy is a plain number with no direction. The total is just the sum of the pair terms, with their signs.

    \(U = U_{12} + U_{23} + U_{13}\)
    \(= (-0.4046) + (-0.5057) + (0.1348)\)
    \(= -0.775\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansNegative: the attractions outweigh the repulsion. It would take \(0.775\ \mathrm{J}\) of outside work to pull all three charges apart to infinity.

  9. Part (b): decide which pairs are new

    WhyBefore \(q_3\) arrives, \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are already sitting there, so their pair energy \(U_{12}\) already exists. \(q_3\) starts at infinity, where every pair it belongs to has zero energy. Bringing it in creates exactly the pairs that contain \(q_3\).

    \(\Delta U\) \(= U_{\text{after}} - U_{\text{before}}\)
    \(= (U_{12} + U_{23} + U_{13}) - U_{12}\)
    \(= U_{23} + U_{13}\)

    Means\(U_{12}\) cancels. Leaving it in is the most common mistake on part (b).

  10. Add the two new terms: this is part (b)

    \(\Delta U = (-0.5057) + (0.1348)\) \(= -0.371\ \mathrm{J}\)

    MeansNegative: \(q_2\) pulls \(q_3\) in harder than \(q_1\) pushes it away, so the field itself does positive work bringing \(q_3\) in.

    Copper shell: inner radius \(b = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}\), outer radius \(c = 3.0\ \mathrm{cm}\), charge \(+Q = +8.0\ \mathrm{nC}\).

  2. Convert to SI units

    Why"n" is \(10^{-9}\); centimeters divide by 100.

    \(q = 3.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(Q = 8.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}\); \(a = 0.010\), \(b = 0.020\), \(c = 0.030\ \mathrm{m}\)

  3. Charge on the copper's inner surface

    WhyDraw a Gaussian sphere inside the copper metal. The field in metal is zero, so the flux is zero, so by Gauss's law the charge enclosed must be zero. It already contains the aluminum's \(-q\).

    \(-3.0\ \mathrm{nC} + q_{\text{inner}}\) \(= 0\) \(\Rightarrow q_{\text{inner}}\) \(= +3.0\ \mathrm{nC}\)

  4. Charge on the copper's outer surface

    WhyThe copper's total is \(+Q\). Whatever is not on the inner surface is on the outer surface.

    \(q_{\text{outer}}\) \(= Q - q\) \(= 8.0 - 3.0\) \(= 5.0\ \mathrm{nC}\)

    MeansThe outside world sees only this net charge.

  5. V outside the copper

    WhyOutside a spherically symmetric set of charges, everything acts like one point charge at the center, holding the total enclosed: \(-q + Q\). So the given \(V = kQ/r\) applies with the net charge.

    \(k(Q - q)\) \(= (8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})\) \(= 44.95\ \mathrm{V\,m}\)
    \(V(r) = \dfrac{44.95}{r}\) \((r \gt c)\)

  6. V in the copper

    WhyIn metal \(E = 0\), so \(V\) does not change from its value at the outer surface \(r = c\).

    \(V_{\text{copper}}\) \(= \dfrac{44.95}{0.030}\) \(= 1498\ \mathrm{V}\)

  7. The field in the gap

    WhyA Gaussian sphere of radius \(r\) in the gap encloses only the aluminum's \(-q\). Negative \(E_r\) means the field points inward, toward the negative sphere.

    \(E_r = -\dfrac{kq}{r^2},\) \(kq\) \(= (8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-9})\) \(= 26.97\ \mathrm{V\,m}\)

  8. V in the gap, walking inward from the copper

    WhyStart at \(r = b\), where \(V\) equals the copper's value, and apply \(\Delta V = -\int\vec E\cdot d\vec l\) inward. The integral of \(1/r^2\) is \(-1/r\).

    \(V(r)\) \(= V_{\text{copper}} - \int_b^r\left(-\dfrac{kq}{r'^2}\right)dr'\) \(= V_{\text{copper}} - kq\left(\dfrac1r - \dfrac1b\right)\)

    MeansAs \(r\) shrinks, \(1/r - 1/b\) grows, so \(V\) drops. Walking toward a negative charge lowers the potential. ✓

  9. V inside the aluminum

    WhyWalk the gap all the way in to \(r = a\). Inside the aluminum \(E = 0\), so every point there has that same value.

    \(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\) \(= \dfrac{1}{0.010} - \dfrac{1}{0.020}\) \(= 50.00\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\)
    \(kq(\ldots) = (26.97)(50.00)\) \(= 1349\ \mathrm{V}\)
    \(V_{\text{Al}} = 1498 - 1349\) \(= 150\ \mathrm{V}\)

    MeansLower than the copper, because the aluminum is the negative one. Still positive here, because the net positive charge outside lifts everything.

7 · The last look before the quiz

  • Read the given equations first; match every letter to a number in the problem or the figure before you compute anything.
  • Read every number, then convert units before anything else: μC, nC, μg, mg, cm.
  • \(U = kq_1q_2/r\): signs in, \(r\) not squared, one term per pair, zero at infinity.
  • Released from rest: \(\tfrac12mv^2 = U_i - U_f\); convert μg and mg to kg before anything else.
  • \(V = kq/r\) is a plain number; add with signs; \(\Delta U = q\Delta V\); the field's work is \(-q\Delta V\), yours is \(+q\Delta V\).
  • Same distance from a point charge means same \(V\) and zero work, whatever the path.
  • Positive charges move to lower \(V\); negative charges to higher \(V\); moving along an equipotential costs no work at all.
  • \(\Delta V = -Ed\) in a uniform field; \(E\) is minus the slope of \(V\); \(E = 0\) in a conductor means \(V\) is constant there, not zero.
  • Gauss then integrate: sphere \(kQ_{\text{enc}}/r^2 \to kQ_{\text{enc}}/r\); line \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r) \to -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(r/r_0)\); plates \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0 \to\) linear \(V\).
  • Coaxial cylinders: \(\ln(b/a)\), never \(c\). Nested shells: inner surface takes \(-(\text{enclosed})\), outer surface takes the rest; \(V\) in the metal is \(k(\text{net})/c\).
  • Constants will usually be printed too; if not: \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\), \(e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\), \(2k = 1/(2\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.80\times10^{10}\).
