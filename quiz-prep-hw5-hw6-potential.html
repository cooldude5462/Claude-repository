2 · The two ideas, from zero

2.1 Work, and why a "potential energy" exists at all

Work is force times the distance moved along the force: \(W = Fd\cos\theta\), in joules. When a force helps the motion (\(\theta \lt 90^\circ\)) its work is positive; when it fights the motion its work is negative. The work-energy theorem from Physics 2111 says the total work by all forces equals the change in kinetic energy, \(W_{\text{total}} = \Delta K\).

Some forces, gravity and the Coulomb force among them, have a special property: the work they do between two points does not depend on the path. For such a force you can define a potential energy \(U\) that only depends on where things are, with

\[ \Delta U = U_{\text{final}} - U_{\text{initial}} = -W_{\text{field}} \]
What each letter means
\(W\)
work done by a force, in joules (J)
\(F,\ d,\ \theta\)
force magnitude (N), distance moved (m), angle between the force and the motion
\(\Delta K\)
change in kinetic energy, \(\tfrac12mv_f^2 - \tfrac12mv_i^2\), in J
\(U,\ \Delta U\)
electric potential energy and its change (final minus initial), in J
\(W_{\text{field}}\)
work done by the electric force (not by your hand), in J
Why the minus sign

Lift a ball. Gravity pulls down while the ball moves up, so gravity's work is negative, and the ball's potential energy goes up. Same rule for charges: when the electric force does negative work (the charge is pushed against the field's wishes), \(U\) increases; when the field does positive work (the charge goes where the field pushes it), \(U\) decreases. Lecture 5's slogan: "Nature wants things to move in such a way that PE decreases." A charge released from rest always starts moving in the direction that lowers \(U\).

Your hand is different. It is not a conservative force, so it has no potential energy of its own. When you carry a charge at constant speed, \(\Delta K = 0\), so your work exactly cancels the field's: \(W_{\text{hand}} = -W_{\text{field}} = +\Delta U\). Whatever energy you put in is stored as \(U\).

2.2 Potential energy of two point charges

\[ U = \frac{k\,q_1 q_2}{r} \qquad (U = 0 \text{ when the charges are infinitely far apart}) \]
What each letter means
\(U\)
the potential energy stored in the pair, in J; it belongs to the system, not to one charge
\(k\)
Coulomb's constant, \(8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}\)
\(q_1,\ q_2\)
the two charges with their signs, in coulombs (C); \(1\ \mu\mathrm{C} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(1\ \mathrm{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}\)
\(r\)
the distance between them, in meters; it is not squared (the force has \(r^2\), the energy has \(r\))
Why 1/r, and why the sign matters

The Coulomb force falls as \(1/r^2\). Potential energy is the work needed to bring the charges in from infinity, and adding up (integrating) a \(1/r^2\) force over distance gives \(1/r\). Keep the signs of the charges in the formula: a positive product means like charges (a hill, \(U \gt 0\)), a negative product means opposite charges (a well, \(U \lt 0\)). Lecture 5's clickers: two opposite charges at \(d\) have lower energy than at \(2d\) (deeper in the well), while two negative charges at \(d\) have higher energy than at \(2d\) (higher on the hill). "Highest potential energy" is the answer to both questions but for opposite reasons.

Change in \(U\) when one charge moves. If \(+q\) goes from distance \(r_1\) to distance \(r_2\) from a fixed \(+Q\):

\[ \Delta U = kQq\left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1}\right) \]

This is the Lecture 5 checkpoint (answer D). Only the starting and ending distances matter; the straight-line distance actually traveled, \(R\), never appears, because the force is conservative. Moving away (\(r_2 \gt r_1\)) makes \(\Delta U\) negative for like charges, as it must.

2.3 Many charges: one term per pair

\[ U_{\text{total}} = \sum_{\text{every pair}}\frac{k\,q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} \]
What each letter means
\(\sum_{\text{every pair}}\)
add one term for each pair of charges, counting each pair once: 3 charges make 3 pairs, 4 charges make 6 pairs
\(q_i,\ q_j\)
the two charges in that pair, with signs, in C
\(r_{ij}\)
the distance between those two charges, in m

\(U_{\text{total}}\) is the work an outside agent must do to assemble the whole arrangement from charges that start infinitely far apart. Lecture 5 builds it one charge at a time: the first charge costs nothing (nothing is there yet), the second costs its pair with the first, the third costs its pairs with both of the others, and so on. Two consequences you will use:

  • Bringing in one more charge \(q\) (the others already in place) changes \(U\) by the sum of the new pairs only, which is \(\Delta U = qV\) where \(V\) is the potential the existing charges make at the new spot (HW5 P1 part b, HW5 P5, Fall 2018 Q4).
  • Pairs between fixed charges never change while a third charge moves, so leave them out of any energy-conservation calculation (HW5 P6).

2.4 Energy conservation: the speed of a released charge

The Coulomb force is conservative, so when nothing else does work (no hand, no friction), mechanical energy is conserved:

\[ K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tfrac12 m v_f^2 = U_i - U_f \ \text{ (released from rest)} \]
What each letter means
\(K_i,\ K_f\)
initial and final kinetic energy, \(\tfrac12mv^2\), in J; \(K_i = 0\) when released from rest
\(U_i,\ U_f\)
potential energy at the starting and ending positions, from \(kQq/r\) (summed over the moving charge's pairs), in J
\(m\)
mass of the moving charge, in kg: \(1\ \mu\mathrm{g} = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{kg}\), \(1\ \mathrm{mg} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{kg}\)
\(v_f\)
its speed at the end, \(v_f = \sqrt{2(U_i - U_f)/m}\), in m/s

Steps that never change: (1) write \(U\) at the start, (2) write \(U\) at the end, (3) the difference is the kinetic energy gained, (4) \(v = \sqrt{2K/m}\). If the charge is released from rest and \(U_i - U_f\) comes out negative, you have the direction wrong: a charge released from rest can only move toward lower \(U\). Only when both charges are free (Lecture 5, Example 5.1) do you also need momentum conservation to split the energy between them.

2.5 Electric potential: energy per coulomb

The potential energy of a charge \(q\) in someone else's field is always proportional to \(q\). Divide it out and what remains is a property of the location alone, the electric potential:

\[ V = \frac{U}{q}, \qquad V_{\text{point charge}} = \frac{kq}{r}, \qquad V_{\text{several}} = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]
What each letter means
\(V\)
electric potential at a point, in volts (1 V = 1 J/C); a plain number, no direction
\(U\)
the potential energy a charge \(q\) has at that point, in J; so \(U = qV\)
\(q\) (in \(kq/r\))
the source charge making the potential, with its sign: a negative charge makes negative \(V\)
\(r,\ r_i\)
distance from the source charge to the point, in m
\(\sum_i\)
add the numbers \(kq_i/r_i\) for each source charge; no components, no angles ("no sines or cosines," Lecture 6)

The two relationships you will use constantly:

\[ \Delta U = q\,\Delta V, \qquad W_{\text{field}} = -q\,\Delta V, \qquad W_{\text{you}} = +q\,\Delta V \]
Why potential is useful, and how to read its sign

Field is force per coulomb; potential is energy per coulomb. Both describe the space, not the test charge. Around a positive charge \(V\) is a hill (positive, higher near the charge); around a negative charge it is a well. A positive charge rolls downhill, toward lower \(V\). A negative charge does the opposite: its energy \(qV\) is lowest where \(V\) is highest, so it moves toward higher \(V\). Lecture 6's Example 6.1 makes the point: flipping the sign of \(q\) flips the force, the works, and \(\Delta U\), but \(\Delta V\) stays \(+2.4\ \mathrm{V}\); the potential difference belongs to the field, not to the charge.

2.6 Equipotentials: same distance, same potential, zero work

All points at the same distance from a point charge have the same \(V\). Moving a charge between two such points changes nothing: \(\Delta V = 0\), so \(\Delta U = 0\) and the field does no work, no matter how long or curved the path. Surfaces of constant \(V\) are called equipotentials; for a point charge they are spheres, for a long line they are cylinders, for a large flat sheet they are planes. The field is always perpendicular to them and points from high \(V\) to low \(V\). A conductor in equilibrium has \(E = 0\) inside, so its entire volume is one equipotential (Lecture 6 checkpoint: zero field means constant \(V\), not zero \(V\)). Equipotential lines drawn at equal voltage steps are closest together where the field is strongest.

2.7 Potential from the field, and field from the potential

\[ V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \vec E\cdot d\vec l, \qquad \text{uniform field along the motion: } \Delta V = -Ed, \qquad E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx} \]
What each letter means
\(V_a,\ V_b\)
potential at the start and end points, in V
\(\vec E\)
the electric field along the path, in N/C = V/m
\(d\vec l\)
a tiny step along the path, in m; the dot product keeps the part of the step along \(\vec E\)
\(d\)
distance moved along the field direction, in m
\(E_x = -dV/dx\)
the field is minus the slope of \(V\); it points "downhill," from high \(V\) to low \(V\)

Walking with the field lowers \(V\); walking against it raises \(V\); walking perpendicular to it changes nothing. On a graph of \(V\) versus \(x\), the field is strongest where the curve is steepest, and it points in \(-x\) wherever the slope is positive (Lecture 6 checkpoints).

Why a conductor is flat in V, and why lines cannot use infinity

Inside a conductor in equilibrium the field is zero, and \(\Delta V = -\int E\,dr\) adds up nothing across it, so \(V\) holds the value it had at the outer surface all the way through (HW6 P1's copper shell, P2's aluminum interior). For an infinite line the field falls only as \(1/r\), and \(\int dr/r = \ln r\) grows without limit, so "zero at infinity" is impossible; the problem hands you a reference radius instead, and the potential at any \(r\) is minus the log of the ratio \(r/r_0\).

HW6 Problem 5 · Two charged plates: field and potential in three regions most likely
Concept: sheet fields add or cancel · \(\Delta V = -Ed\) in a uniform field · \(V = 0\) where the problem says
Plate at \(y = 0\) with \(-\sigma\) (defined as \(V = 0\)); plate at \(y = d\) with \(+\sigma\). Find \(\vec E\) and \(V\) for \(y \lt 0\), \(0 \lt y \lt d\), \(y \gt d\); then the equipotential and field-line statements.
Recipe (any signs, any numbers)
  1. Each plate alone: \(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\), pointing away from a positive plate and toward a negative one, the same on both sides.
  2. In each of the three regions, add the two contributions with signs along \(\hat j\): they cancel outside the pair and add to \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) between.
  3. Start \(V\) at the plate the problem declares to be zero and integrate \(-E_y\,dy\) across the gap; \(V\) is flat wherever \(E = 0\).
  1. Field of each plate. A large sheet makes a uniform field of size \(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\) on both sides: away from a positive sheet, toward a negative one.
  2. Left region, \(y \lt 0\). The \(-\sigma\) plate is to the right and pulls toward itself: \(+\hat j\). The \(+\sigma\) plate is also to the right and pushes away: \(-\hat j\). Equal sizes, opposite directions: \(\vec E = 0\).
  3. Between, \(0 \lt y \lt d\). The \(-\sigma\) plate is below and pulls down (\(-\hat j\)); the \(+\sigma\) plate is above and pushes down (\(-\hat j\)). They add: \(\vec E = -\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat j\).
  4. Right region, \(y \gt d\). Both plates are below; \(-\sigma\) pulls down, \(+\sigma\) pushes up: \(\vec E = 0\).
  5. Potential, starting from the zero. \(V(0) = 0\) at the negative plate. Moving from \(0\) to \(y\) inside the gap goes against the field (field points \(-y\), we move \(+y\)), so \(V\) rises: \(V(y) = -\int_0^y E_y\,dy' = -\int_0^y\left(-\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\right)dy' = +\dfrac{\sigma y}{\varepsilon_0}\).
  6. Outside. Where \(E = 0\), \(V\) does not change. Left of the negative plate: \(V = 0\) (same as the plate). Right of the positive plate: \(V = \dfrac{\sigma d}{\varepsilon_0}\), the value reached at \(y = d\).
Answers (a) \(\vec E = 0\) · (b) \(\vec E = -\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat j\) · (c) \(\vec E = 0\) · (d) \(V = 0\) · (e) \(V = \dfrac{\sigma y}{\varepsilon_0}\) · (f) \(V = \dfrac{\sigma d}{\varepsilon_0}\).

(g) Equipotentials, true statements: the whole volume left of the negative plate (\(V = 0\) everywhere there); the whole volume right of the positive plate (\(V = \sigma d/\varepsilon_0\)); between the plates, planes perpendicular to the \(y\) axis (each such plane has one \(y\), hence one \(V\)). False: the volume between the plates (\(V\) changes with \(y\)); line segments parallel to \(y\) (\(V\) changes along them); planes perpendicular to \(x\) or \(z\) (they span all \(y\) values).

(h) Field lines, true statements: where the field is nonzero the lines are parallel to the \(y\) axis; the density is greatest between the plates (the only place the field is nonzero); lines originate at \(y = d\) (the positive plate) and terminate at \(y = 0\) (the negative plate). False: parallel to \(x\) or \(z\); greatest density outside; originate at \(y = 0\).

Concept: convert \(\sigma\) on a pipe to \(\lambda\) · same log · adding a constant moves the zero
Long metal pipe, radius \(a\), surface charge density \(\sigma\); \(V = 0\) at \(r = 3a\). Find \(E_r\) outside and inside, \(V\) outside and inside, and the constant \(V_{\text{shift}}\) that moves the zero to \(r = 2a\).
Recipe (any reference radius, any shift)
  1. \(\lambda = 2\pi a\sigma\), so outside \(E_r = \sigma a/(\varepsilon_0 r)\); inside 0.
  2. Outside: \(V = -\dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{r}{r_0}\); inside: \(\dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{r_0}{a}\).
  3. To move the zero to a new radius \(r_1\): \(V_{\text{shift}} = -V_{\text{old}}(r_1) = \dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{r_1}{r_0}\).
  1. Charge per length. A length \(L\) of pipe has area \(2\pi aL\) and charge \(\sigma\,2\pi aL\), so \(\lambda = 2\pi a\sigma\).
  2. (a) Outside. \(E_r = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \dfrac{2\pi a\sigma}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0 r}\). Check at the surface: \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\), the conductor-surface rule.
  3. (b) Inside. \(E_r = 0\) (no charge enclosed; metal).
  4. (c) \(V\) outside. \(V(r) = -\displaystyle\int_{3a}^{r}\frac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0 r'}dr' = -\frac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{r}{3a}\).
  5. (d) \(V\) inside. Constant at the surface value: \(-\dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac{a}{3a} = -\dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln\dfrac13 = \dfrac{\sigma a}{\varepsilon_0}\ln 3\).
  6. (e) Moving the zero. HW6 Problem 1 · Aluminum sphere (\(-q\)) inside a copper shell (\(+Q\)) possible
Concept: Gauss's law region by region, then integrate inward from infinity · conductors are flat
Solid aluminum sphere, radius \(a\), charge \(-q\). Concentric copper shell, inner radius \(b\), outer radius \(c\), charge \(+Q\), with \(Q \gt q\). \(V(\infty) = 0\). Find \(E_r\) and \(V\) in \(r \gt c\), \(b \lt r \lt c\), \(a \lt r \lt b\), \(r \lt a\); then the equipotential and field-line statements.
Recipe (any charges)
  1. Surface charges: inner surface of the shell takes minus the enclosed charge; the outer surface takes the rest.
  2. \(E_r = k(\text{enclosed})/r^2\) in each gap or outside; 0 in each metal. Use \(1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\) for \(k\) when \(k\) is not on the variable list.
  3. \(V\) outside \(= k(\text{net})/r\); frozen through each metal; across a gap, \(V(r) = V(\text{outer edge}) - \int E_r\,dr\).
  1. Surface charges first. Aluminum surface: \(-q\). Copper inner surface: \(+q\) (a Gaussian sphere in the copper metal has \(E = 0\), hence encloses zero). Copper outer surface: \(Q - q\).
  2. (a) \(r \gt c\). Enclosed \(= -q + Q\): \(E_r = \dfrac{Q - q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\), outward since \(Q \gt q\).
  3. (b) \(b \lt r \lt c\). Inside the copper: \(E_r = 0\).
  4. (c) \(a \lt r \lt b\). Enclosed \(= -q\): \(E_r = -\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\) (inward).
  5. (d) \(r \lt a\). Inside the aluminum: \(E_r = 0\).
  6. (e) \(V\) for \(r \gt c\). Looks like a point charge \(Q - q\): \(V = \dfrac{Q - q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\).
  7. (f) \(V\) for \(b \lt r \lt c\). No field, so \(V\) is frozen at its value at \(r = c\): \(V = \dfrac{Q - q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c}\).
  8. (g) \(V\) for \(a \lt r \lt b\). Start at \(r = b\) with the copper's value and integrate inward through the inward field: \(V(r) = V(b) - \displaystyle\int_b^r E_r\,dr' = \frac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c} - \int_b^r\left(-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r'^2}\right)dr' = \frac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c} - \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac1r - \frac1b\right)\). Walking inward toward a negative charge, \(V\) decreases, and this expression does decrease as \(r\) shrinks. ✓
  9. (h) \(V\) for \(r \lt a\). Frozen at the aluminum's surface value: \(V = \dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c} - \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\right)\).
Answers \(E_r\): \(\dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\), 0, \(-\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\), 0. \(V\): \(\dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\), \(\dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c}\), \(\dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c} - \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1b\right)\), \(\dfrac{Q-q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 c} - \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1a-\dfrac1b\right)\).

(i) Equipotentials, true: the whole copper volume \(b \lt r \lt c\); spherical surfaces in the gap \(a \lt r \lt b\); the whole aluminum volume \(r \lt a\); spherical surfaces outside, \(r \gt c\). False: the whole gap volume and the whole outside volume (\(V\) varies with \(r\) there); radial lines (\(V\) changes along them).

(j) Field lines, true: some lines originate on the copper's inner surface (\(+q\)) and terminate on the aluminum's surface (\(-q\)); some originate on the copper's outer surface (\(Q - q \gt 0\)) and extend to infinity; the line density is greater in \(a \lt r \lt b\) than in \(b \lt r \lt c\) (where it is zero). False: circular lines anywhere (field lines are radial; circles are equipotentials); lines from the copper's outer surface to its inner surface (they would cross the metal, where \(E = 0\)); lines originating on the aluminum (a negative charge is where lines end); lines coming in from infinity (the net charge is positive, so lines go out).

7 · The last look before the quiz

  • Read every number, then convert units before anything else: μC, nC, μg, mg, cm.
  • \(U = kq_1q_2/r\): signs in, \(r\) not squared, one term per pair, zero at infinity.
  • Released from rest: \(\tfrac12mv^2 = U_i - U_f\); convert μg and mg to kg before anything else.
  • \(V = kq/r\) is a plain number; add with signs; \(\Delta U = q\Delta V\); the field's work is \(-q\Delta V\), yours is \(+q\Delta V\).
  • Same distance from a point charge means same \(V\) and zero work, whatever the path.
  • Positive charges move to lower \(V\); negative charges to higher \(V\); moving along an equipotential costs no work at all.
  • \(\Delta V = -Ed\) in a uniform field; \(E\) is minus the slope of \(V\); \(E = 0\) in a conductor means \(V\) is constant there, not zero.
  • Gauss then integrate: sphere \(kQ_{\text{enc}}/r^2 \to kQ_{\text{enc}}/r\); line \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r) \to -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(r/r_0)\); plates \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0 \to\) linear \(V\).
  • Coaxial cylinders: \(\ln(b/a)\), never \(c\). Nested shells: inner surface takes \(-(\text{enclosed})\), outer surface takes the rest; \(V\) in the metal is \(k(\text{net})/c\).
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\), \(e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\), \(2k = 1/(2\pi\varepsilon_0) = 1.80\times10^{10}\).
